Foundations of human civilization

Read time labels

BC and BCE number the same years before the common era. AD and CE number the same years of the common era. Circa, written c. or ca., means about. A decade is ten years. A century is one hundred years. Within BCE, the larger year number is earlier.

Worked example. Around 10,000 BCE comes before 3500 BCE. The dates are about 6,500 years apart, or about 65 centuries.

From gathering food to growing it

Nomadic hunter-gatherers moved as food and water became available in different places. Hunting tools helped obtain food, cutting tools helped process food and hides, fire provided warmth and helped cook food, and shelters offered protection. Different groups built different kinds of shelters and moved at different times.

Over many generations, people domesticated plants and animals, raising them for useful traits. Farming helped many groups stay near their fields in permanent settlements. Stored food surpluses could feed people who specialized as weavers, builders, or potters. Plant fibers and wool provided clothing materials. Settled villages could build shelters from materials such as wood or mud brick. Barter means exchanging goods or services directly.

Worked example. A farmer swaps spare grain for a pot to store food. Extra grain is a surplus, pot-making is a specialized job, and the swap is barter.

Mesopotamia and Sumer

Use evidence to recognize seven characteristics

Stable food supply
Farms, plows, and records of barley can show how communities produced and managed food.
Social structure
Artisans made goods and people of higher rank held different roles and privileges. Comparing tools, homes, and burials can give clues to differences in roles and status.
Government
Rulers, laws, taxes, and armies show how leaders organized work, made rules, and defended cities.
Religion
Temple settings and statues used as offerings can provide evidence about worship.
Culture and art
Jewelry, decorated cups, and sculpture show craftsmanship and ideas people valued.
Technology
Wheels helped move goods and irrigation channels brought water to fields. Pottery helped store food and arches helped span openings.
Writing
Scribes pressed wedge-shaped marks into clay. Cuneiform helped people keep records and share information.

Worked example. A tablet listing amounts of barley is evidence of writing and recordkeeping. The barley also gives a clue about the food supply. Match the clue to the claim the question asks you to support.

Read the map key

Try it with the classroom map. Use page 4 to trace the Euphrates west of the Tigris. On page 5, find Nineveh near the Tigris, then follow the river system south toward Babylon and Ur. Cover the reference pages and locate the three city dots on page 1. Use page 3 for the assignment colors.

The Tigris and Euphrates are rivers. The Mediterranean Sea, Red Sea, and Persian Gulf are larger bodies of water. The Zagros Mountains are east of the Mesopotamian lowlands. The Syrian Desert lies west of much of Mesopotamia. The Fertile Crescent is a region that includes Mesopotamia and curves west toward the eastern Mediterranean.

On the classroom map, use stars for Sumer, Akkad, Assyria, and Babylonia and dots for Ur, Nineveh, and Babylon. Babylon is a city and Babylonia is a larger territory. Color water and trace rivers in blue, color the Fertile Crescent green and the Syrian Desert light brown, and use brown triangles for the Zagros. Label the desert and mountains in black and add the colors and symbols to the key.

Worked example. The assigned reading explains that river floods could leave fertile silt, but summers were dry. A canal carried river water to the fields during the dry season. More dependable harvests supported larger settlements and eventually city-states, each with its own government and surrounding territory.

Egypt · The Nile, writing, and the Great Pyramid

Kemet means Black Land, a name associated with the dark, fertile land by the Nile. The Red Land was the desert. The roughly 4,000-mile Nile flows north. Upper Egypt is upstream in the south, near Thebes. Lower Egypt is downstream in the north, where sediment built up to form a delta. Rocky rapids called cataracts made travel harder.

Flooding brought water and fertile silt. People used basins to hold floodwater and ditches and canals to move it. Wheat and barley were staple crops. Seasonal patterns and observations of the sky helped mark the flood season. Egypt’s New Year was associated with the start of the flood season. Akhet was the flood season, Peret the growing season, and Shemu the harvest season.

A scribe was trained to read, write, and keep records. The menhed was a writing kit. Hieroglyphs are signs that can represent sounds, words, or ideas. Papyrus grew along the Nile, and people used it to make writing sheets, boats, and footwear. Egyptian gods could be shown with animal features. These figures are clues about religious beliefs.

Narmer is commonly associated with the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt. Menes is the traditional name for Egypt’s first king, and historians debate how the names are related. The Egypt vocabulary chapter covers the assigned terms, with the binder review also using dynasty for a line of rulers from the same family. Hatshepsut ruled as pharaoh during the New Kingdom.

Khufu’s Great Pyramid at Giza was an Old Kingdom tomb built in the 26th century BCE. Egypt’s Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities estimates 10 to 20 years of construction and about 3,800 years as the world’s tallest structure. A commonly cited estimate is about 2.3 million blocks. Typical limestone blocks weighed a few tons, while some granite pieces were much heavier. Most of the structure is limestone. Granite for the king’s burial chamber came from Aswan. Archaeologists are still studying how the builders raised and placed the blocks. The Valley of the Kings near Thebes contains rock-cut royal tombs from the New Kingdom, including Tutankhamun’s.

Worked example. Finding Aswan granite in Khufu’s chamber helps identify the stone’s source. The granite alone cannot tell us which route or lifting method the builders used.

Ancient Israel · Migrations, kingdoms, and traditions

In biblical tradition, Abraham traveled from Mesopotamia to Canaan and entered a covenant, or binding agreement, with God. Abraham’s grandson Jacob was also called Israel, and his descendants were associated with the tribes of Israel. A famine led the people to Egypt. The Exodus is the story of Moses leading the Israelites out of slavery in Egypt, through Sinai, and toward the Promised Land. Joshua leads the people into Canaan in the biblical account, and judges lead before the monarchy.

Monotheism means belief in one God. The Torah is the first part of the Tanakh, the Hebrew Bible. In the biblical story, Moses receives the Ten Commandments at Mount Sinai. Prophets delivered messages of warning and hope.

Remember the sequence Saul → David → Solomon. David made Jerusalem his capital, and Solomon built the First Temple in the biblical account. After the kingdom split, Israel was in the north and Judah was in the south. Assyria took the northern kingdom around 722 BCE. Babylon later took Judah and Jerusalem and sent many people into exile. In 539 BCE, Cyrus of Persia took Babylon and subsequently permitted exiles to return. Some returned and the Temple was rebuilt. Others remained in communities outside Judah.

A diaspora is a people living spread out away from their homeland. Synagogues became places for gathering, worship, and study. Keeping the Sabbath and following kosher food laws were ways of practicing Judaism. The Maccabean revolt and rededication of the Temple are remembered at Hanukkah. Rome later destroyed the Second Temple. Rabbinic teaching helped Jewish communities maintain and develop religious life, and the Talmud records generations of rabbinic discussion.

Worked example. “Moses leads people out of Egypt” describes the Exodus in biblical tradition. “Babylon takes people from Judah into exile” describes one event that contributed to the diaspora.
